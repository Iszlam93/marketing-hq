# Competitive research

## When to use
- A battlecard or positioning request needs facts about rivals.
- I need to find an open claim, or check that a planned claim is not already owned.
- A competitor moved (acquisition, redirect, new pricing) and the picture may be stale.

## Inputs
- Product file (what it does, audience, approved claims, claims to avoid).
- The brief: which competitors, which buyer, which market.
- Optional: ad library results the brief points to.

## Method
1. **Pick the set.** Choose 5 to 12 competitors across three groups: direct (same job), adjacent (same buyer, different job) and substitutes (build it yourself, do nothing). Name them and give one line on why each is in. Ask what the buyer would use instead.
2. **Use one fixed card per competitor.** Record from the live public pages: headline and sub-headline (short quote with link), three main claims, who the page speaks to, whether it speaks to the target industry or regulation, how it treats the key value themes (headline / mentioned / absent), pricing as listed, and proof offered (case studies, logos, numbers). Record the date of access.
3. **Check the page is current.** Open each page. Note redirects, rebrands and acquisitions. If a page is only seen through a summary or a search snippet, say so. Re-check any quote before it is used in public.
4. **Check ads.** Search the public ad libraries (for example the LinkedIn Ad Library) for each competitor and for key phrases. Record what they promise and what none of them says. Absence in an ad library is evidence of absence only for that library and date.
5. **Compare.** Build a grid: competitors down, value themes across, each cell headline / mentioned / absent. Count how many are in each cell.
6. **Find the open ground.** Three questions: which claim is crowded (many rivals, same words)? which claim is owned (one rival, strong proof)? which claim is open and true for the product? An open claim must be supported by an approved product claim. If it is not, mark it **Hypothesis**.
7. **Write the "do not say" list.** Claims the product should avoid because a rival owns them or because the product cannot prove them. Include claims on the product file's "Claims to avoid".
8. **Write threats and answers.** For each main rival: where they win, where I win, the likely objection a buyer raises, and a short honest answer. Never state a rival weakness unless a source shows it.
9. **State confidence** and what is not known.

## Quality checks
- [ ] Each competitor has a card with sources and access dates.
- [ ] Quotes are under 15 words, with a link, and any quote seen only in a summary is flagged.
- [ ] Rebrands, redirects and acquisitions are checked and stated.
- [ ] The grid uses only headline / mentioned / absent, with the page cited.
- [ ] The open claim is backed by an approved product claim, or marked Hypothesis.
- [ ] No rival weakness is claimed without a source.
- [ ] A "do not say" list exists.
- [ ] Observed and inferred are labelled apart.

## Worked example (Kimchi, Oct 2026; example only)
A scan of 12 competitor sites found that rivals report AI spend after it happens ("AI spend per developer went from $63 to $1,314"). An ad-library check found no one saying budgets are enforced before the spend. The open ground was "control before, not reports after". The crowded ground was "proving value" and "any model". Source: `docs/kimchi/competitor-messaging.md`, `docs/kimchi/value-tally.md`, `docs/kimchi/ad-library-reviews.md`.

## Why a PMM does this
Positioning means choosing where to stand relative to alternatives. A PMM cannot choose well without knowing what rivals already say. The valuable finding is rarely "who is best". It is "which true claim is nobody making". I also write the "do not say" list because a claim that someone else owns costs money and wins nothing.
