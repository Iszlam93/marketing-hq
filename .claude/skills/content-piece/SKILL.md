# Content piece (blog or LinkedIn)

## When to use
- A request asks for a blog post, article, LinkedIn post or short thread.
- Positioning and evidence exist (`dependsOn`).
- Not for email (use email-sequence). Not for a sales page (use custom-one-pager).

## Inputs
- Positioning asset and any research asset.
- Product file: approved claims, claims to avoid.
- The brief: audience, channel, the point to make, length.
- For posts in Islam's voice: the `personal-branding` skill (brand book v2.1, in the Iszlam93/personal-branding repo) if available, and his own answers where the brief supplies them.

## Method
1. **Find the one point.** Write it in one sentence a reader could repeat. If it needs "and", it is two pieces. Say which evidence supports it.
2. **Choose the format.**
   - Blog: 500 to 900 words. A headline, a short opening that states the problem, three sections, a close.
   - LinkedIn: 120 to 220 words, a first line that works alone, short paragraphs, no list of hashtags, no emoji strings.
3. **Open with something specific.** A fact, a number with its source, or a short scene from the evidence. Not a question to the whole world, and not "In today's fast-paced world".
4. **Build the body from evidence.** Each paragraph carries one claim and its source. Mix observed (a source says it) with inferred (my view), and label which is which in plain words ("The ads say X. My reading is Y.").
5. **Write in the right voice.** In Islam's posts use "I", based on what he has said or done. Do not invent his experiences. If a story is needed, write `**[add: Islam's own example]**`. Do not use formula devices (a list of three adjectives, "Here's the thing", rhetorical question openers) unless the voice guide allows them.
6. **Keep to approved claims.** Do not state a product claim that is not in the product file. Never use a claim to avoid. If the piece names a company or person, use public facts only, and check they are correct.
7. **Close with one thing the reader can do or think.** No hard sell.
8. **Headline or first line options.** Give two. State which one is the stronger and why.
9. **Visual note.** One line on any image or chart wanted, with data and source. Do not produce the image.
10. **Fact check table.** List every fact and where it comes from.

## Output template
```
# <Blog | LinkedIn>: <working title>

## Point, reader, channel
## Draft
**Headline / first line (A):** ...
**(B):** ...

<Body>

## Visual note
## Fact check
| Statement | Type (observed / inferred) | Source |

## Open items
- **[add: ...]**

## Sources
```

## Quality checks
- [ ] One point, stated in one sentence.
- [ ] Length matches the channel.
- [ ] Every fact is in the fact-check table with a source.
- [ ] Observed and inferred are labelled in the text.
- [ ] No invented story, quote, number or customer.
- [ ] No claim from "Claims to avoid".
- [ ] "I" is used where Islam speaks; never "we".
- [ ] No filler openers and no stock formula devices.
- [ ] Nothing was posted, scheduled or sent.

## Worked example (Kimchi, Oct 2026; example only)
A post from the job-ad funnel: "I read 15 ads for AI-rollout roles. 11 named governance. 5 named spend limits." The point: buyers are asked to control, so a message about control fits. The numbers came from `docs/kimchi/value-tally.md`; the post would still need `**[add: Islam's own example]**` for any personal story.

## Why a PMM does this
Content is where the positioning meets the public. A PMM writes a few pieces with a clear point of view and real evidence, because they are re-used by sales and social. Showing the fact-check table is a habit that keeps a PMM's name safe: one wrong number can undo a month of credibility.
