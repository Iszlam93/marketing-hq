# Battlecard

## When to use
- A seller or founder will meet a buyer who also considers a named rival.
- Competitive research and positioning assets exist (`dependsOn`).
- Not for public marketing pages. A battlecard is an internal sales aid. Mark it internal.

## Inputs
- Competitive-research asset and positioning asset.
- Product file: approved claims, claims to avoid.
- The brief: the rival, the buyer, the deal stage.

## Method
1. **Name the rival and the situation.** One line on when it comes up ("a buyer already pays for X and asks why switch"). Take the rival facts only from the research asset, with its sources and access dates.
2. **Write the one-line positioning versus this rival**: "Choose the product when ...; choose them when ..." Honest on both sides. A card that never admits where the rival is stronger is not trusted.
3. **Quick facts table.** What each one is for, who it targets, pricing as listed, deployment, proof offered. Every cell has a source. A cell without a source is `**[add: ...]**`.
4. **Where I win, where they win.** Three points each, each tied to a fact. Use "I" or "the product" when Islam speaks. Never claim a weakness of the rival without a source.
5. **Landmine questions.** Three to five questions the seller asks the buyer that expose the gap in a way the buyer notices on their own. Phrase them neutrally. Mark each with the evidence behind it.
6. **Objections and answers.** The top four objections (from forum or call evidence). For each: a short honest answer, a proof point from approved claims, and a line the seller must not say.
7. **Traps and "do not say" list.** Claims the rival owns, claims the product cannot prove, anything in "Claims to avoid".
8. **Proof the seller can show.** Only items that exist and are public. If none, write `**[add: proof]**`.
9. **Freshness.** Put the date checked at the top and list what to re-check (pricing, rebrands, acquisitions) before the card is used.

## Output template
```
# Battlecard: <product> vs <rival>
**Internal use only. Facts checked <date>.**

## When this comes up
## In one line
Choose <product> when ...; choose <rival> when ...

## Quick facts
| | <product> | <rival> |
| For | | |
| Targets | | |
| Pricing | | |
| Deployment | | |
| Proof offered | | |

## Where I win / where they win
**I win when:** 1. 2. 3.
**They win when:** 1. 2. 3.

## Landmine questions
1. <question> (evidence: ...)

## Objections and answers
| Objection | Answer | Proof | Do not say |

## Do not say
## Proof I can show
## Re-check before use
## Open items
- **[add: ...]**

## Sources
```

## Quality checks
- [ ] Marked internal with a check date.
- [ ] Every rival fact is sourced from the research asset or a dated URL.
- [ ] "They win when" is filled in honestly.
- [ ] Landmine questions are neutral and each has evidence.
- [ ] No unsourced claim about the rival; no claim from "Claims to avoid".
- [ ] Each objection has an answer, a proof and a "do not say".
- [ ] Re-check list is present.
- [ ] No "we" in Islam's voice.

## Worked example (Kimchi, Oct 2026; example only)
Against an AI-gateway rival, the honest line was: choose a gateway to route and observe many models; choose the platform when the buyer needs hard limits set before the work runs. A rival's acquisition and a redirect were found during the scan and noted as "re-check before use". Source: `docs/kimchi/competitor-messaging.md`, `docs/kimchi/competitor-summary.md`.

## Why a PMM does this
A battlecard is how PMM work reaches the sales conversation. Sellers do not read long documents; they need the three points that matter at the moment. Being honest about where the rival is better is what makes the seller credible, and that is how the card actually wins deals.
