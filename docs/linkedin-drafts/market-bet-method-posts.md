## Post 1: PMM prompt #NN, "Find who has your problem right now"

**Format:** single prompt card + short caption (1080x1350).

**Hook (cover):** The fastest way to find your next customer is to read who they are hiring.

**Caption**

Most positioning starts with a guess about who the customer is.

There is a faster check. If your product helps someone do a job, then every company hiring for that job is telling you, in public, that they have the problem today.

Job ads are dated, written by the buyer's own company, and free.

Here's the method I use:

→ Name the trigger in one sentence: "A company hiring for [role] has [problem] now."
→ Fix 3 searches before I look at any result. Same region, same time window. No changing them later.
→ Count at every step: all postings → really that role → right industry → right company size.

The last slide has the prompt. Save it.

One rule matters more than the rest: write the searches down first. Otherwise I find what I hoped to find.

What signal do you use to know a company has your problem right now?

## Post 2: Case file #NN (or PMM prompt), "The row most tests leave out"

**Format:** 6 to 7 slide carousel.

**Hook (cover):** Most marketing tests have a plan for winning and a plan for losing. Almost none have a plan for "we can't tell."

**Caption**

Most small marketing tests don't end in a clear win or a clear loss. They end in the middle.

Then the team does the thing people always do: picks the ad that looks best and calls it the winner.

I now write one table before any test starts. It has three rows.

→ **Win:** the rule I set in advance is met. Scale it and keep a control running.
→ **Lose:** it falls clearly short. Drop the message and test the next one.
→ **Can't tell:** it lands in the middle. Run it longer or change one thing. I never pick a winner.

The third row is the one that matters. It's decided while I'm calm, before the numbers arrive.

Slide 4 shows how I set the winner rule and the sample size, with the arithmetic.

Which row does your last test fall into, and did you plan for it?

1. **Cover:** "Your test has 3 possible endings. Most plans cover 2."
2. **The problem:** "Small tests usually end in the middle. Then someone picks the winner they like."
3. **The table:** Win / Lose / Can't tell, each with "what I saw" and "what I do next."
4. **Set the rule before you launch:** "How many people? What gap counts as real? Write it down now." Example: "To tell a 0.5% click rate from 0.7%, each ad needs about 23,000 views (**example**: 95% confidence, 80% power)." Show the working in mono type.

6. **Keep the outcome honest:** "Calls booked is a result I report. It isn't proof the ad caused it."

## Post 3 (short, optional): PMM prompt #NN, "Shows vs Assumes"

**Format:** single card (1080x1350).

**Hook:** I label every finding in a research doc with one word: "Shows" or "Assumes."

**Caption**

A research doc can look certain and still be mostly opinion.

So I tag each line:

→ **Shows:** what the source actually says. Link and date attached.
→ **Assumes:** what I'm inferring from it.

A vendor's website goes under "Shows" only as the vendor's own claim, never as fact.

The surprise: once the lines are tagged, the weakest part of my argument is obvious. It's always the "Assumes" column. And that tells me exactly what to check next.

If a reader disagrees, they can point at one line instead of rejecting the whole doc.

Try it on your last positioning doc. How many lines are really "Assumes"?

#ProductMarketing #AIinMarketing

**Card text:**
> Shows: Job ads for this role mention "missed call-outs" in 6 of 20 (**example**). [link, date]
> Assumes: Buyers care most about dispatch speed.
> → Check next: read 20 more ads, or ask in a trade forum.
