# Presentation script: intro and sections 1–3

About 6 minutes. Speak to the page; don't read the table rows aloud. [Brackets] = what to point at.

---

## Intro (about 45 seconds)

"Thank you for having me.

The brief asked me three things: who Kimchi should go after first, what to say to them, and how to find out fast if I'm wrong. My answer is on two pages. Page one is the bet and the evidence behind it. Page two is what I left out, and a seven-week test that could prove me wrong.

I worked from public sources only, and I used Kimchi myself on the free plan. I also used AI to gather and count sources. I'll say where, and which decisions were mine.

Before the bet, three things I'd change in how Kimchi goes to market today. One: the homepage headline is governance, but its only customer quote praises the Auto router, for keeping frontier quality while using the right model for each task. The proof is about routing; the headline is about control. I'd connect them: routing is control over which model does which work. Two: nobody, Kimchi included, claims the one thing buyers need most, that the limit is set before the work runs, not reported after. Three: the real competitor isn't Claude, it's the new hire's plan to build this in-house. The rest of the list is in appendix D.

I'll take about ten minutes so we have most of the hour for questions."

---

## Section 1: The bet (about 1.5 minutes)

[Point at the headline]

"Here's the message I'd lead with: **'Autonomous agents for your whole team. Limits set before anyone starts.'**

The first half is Kimchi's own goal: moving teams from each developer using AI alone to agents working for the whole team. The second half is the promise: the company decides the limits before the agents run, not after the bill arrives.

[Point at the five boxes]

Who I'd say it to: platform and engineering leaders at UK and EU financial firms, up to about 5,000 staff. Finance comes first because they need control most: 73% of the finance job ads I read name governance as a core duty. Tech runs alongside as a second test group. The trigger is a firm hiring an AI-rollout role. That's the moment someone owns the problem and has a budget, and it's visible on LinkedIn.

[Point at the positioning statement]

In one sentence: Kimchi is where agents work on their own inside limits set first, with budgets per team, developer and agent, the right model for each task, and the choice of your cloud or ours. Unlike Claude Code or Cursor, which limit only their own tool, it covers the whole team, and it's switched on, not built.

[Point at the seller line]

And what a seller would say: 'You're hiring someone to build governed access to coding agents. Kimchi gives them limits, model routing and cost per PR on day one, in our cloud or yours. They start on the rollout, not the plumbing.'

One more thing I want to be clear about: I don't position Kimchi against Claude. Claude is a model and a tool. Kimchi is where a company runs its agents, Claude included."

## Section 2: How I chose (about 1 minute)

"I worked in five steps, in this order.

**First,** I used Kimchi and read its docs. It runs agents that work on their own, and its budgets stop work at the limit, per team, developer and key. That's where both halves of the headline come from: 'autonomous agents for your whole team' and 'limits set before anyone starts'.

**Second,** I looked at the market and the community: Kimchi's own Discord, Reddit and Hacker News, reviews of similar tools, and analysts. I counted which need comes up most. Control came up everywhere.

**Third,** I looked at competitors: who they sell to, and what they say. Each tool limits only its own spend, and their ads talk about AI spend after it happens. None of them leads with team limits set before the work runs. That's the gap.

**Fourth,** I went to job ads on LinkedIn, to see who is actually paying to solve this right now, and where. That pointed to financial services in the UK and EU.

**Fifth,** I chose one main message. Kimchi could lead with several. I didn't lead with price, because only individual users cared, and they left when the free credits ended. I didn't use 'AI engineer', because Devin already owns it. And I didn't lead with data sovereignty, because few buyers mention it in their own words. These stay as supporting points, not the headline."

---

## Section 3: The evidence (about 2.5 minutes)

"The evidence follows the same order: market, then competitors, then job ads.

[Point at ①, the dots]

**First, the market.** Across six kinds of sources, control is the only need that appears in all of them. Proving the value of AI is real too, but Jellyfish and DX already sell exactly that. Model choice is Kilo's message, and price only mattered to individual users. Kimchi's own Discord confirms that: about 1,800 members, mostly individuals who left when the free credits ended. They're not the buyer. And Gartner says AI coding bills are moving from $20–100 a seat to $2,000–5,000 per developer a month, so the pressure is growing.

[Point at ②, the competitor box]

**Second, competitors.** I asked what else this buyer could do. The most common answer is build it in-house: about half the hires are told to, often on a free gateway like LiteLLM. Claude Code, Codex and Copilot have limits, but only for their own tool, and Kimchi can't beat them on models, so it includes them. Devin sells to enterprises and banks with named customers. Kilo is closest in features, and advertises routing and cost. And when they talk about spend, they all talk after the fact. DX: 'AI spend per developer went from $63 to $1,314.' Jellyfish: 'Is your AI spend paying off?' Devin: 'What happens to AI costs when usage doubles?' None leads with team limits set before the work runs. That's the space Kimchi can own.

[Point at ③, the job ads]

**Third, job ads, and why I used them as my main reference.** Kimchi has no proven buyer yet. A job ad is the clearest public sign that a named company is paying to solve this problem now, in its own words. And LinkedIn is where the test can reach the same people. In 90 days, 998 postings came down to 73 real AI-rollout jobs: tech 40%, finance 29%. I read both groups' ads in full. Finance asks for governance far more, 73% against 41%. Tech asks more about value and speed. About half the hires are also asked to evaluate tools, and Kraken's ad says 'evaluating vendors or building internally'. Those are the people Kimchi talks to.

[Point at the bottom row]

When I used Kimchi, I saw budgets per team, user and key, and cost per merged PR. I didn't see a limit stopping real spend, because that needs real usage, and there's no cap per single run yet.

So how sure am I? Medium. The need is clear, and the gap in competitors' messages is clear. What I'm least sure of is whether these firms will buy a platform or build their own. That's exactly what the test on page two is designed to find out."
