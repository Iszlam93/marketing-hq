# Presentation script: Kimchi's first EMEA bet

About 10 minutes, then questions. [Brackets] = what to point at. Speak to the page; don't read the tables.

---

## Opening (about 1 minute)

"Thank you for having me.

The brief asked me three things: who Kimchi should go after first, what to say to them, and how to find out fast if I'm wrong. My answer is on two pages. Page one is the bet and the evidence behind it. Page two is what I left out, and a seven-week test that could prove me wrong. There's an appendix with the next 90 days, how I used AI, and my sources.

I worked from public sources only, and I used Kimchi myself on the free plan.

Before the bet, three things I'd change in how Kimchi goes to market today.

One: the homepage headline is governance, but its only customer quote praises the Auto router, for keeping frontier quality while using the right model for each task. The proof is about routing; the headline is about control. I'd connect them: routing is control over which model does which work.

Two: every tool limits its own spend, and competitors' ads talk about spend after it happens. Nobody, Kimchi included, leads with the thing buyers need most: one set of team limits, set before the work runs.

Three: the real competitor isn't Claude. It's the new hire's plan to build this in-house.

The rest of that list is in appendix D."

---

## Section 1: The bet (about 1.5 minutes)

[Point at the headline]

"Here's the message I'd lead with: **'Autonomous agents for your whole team. Limits set before anyone starts.'**

The first half is Kimchi's own goal: moving teams from each developer using AI alone to agents working for the whole team. The second half is the promise: the company sets the budgets per team and per developer before the agents run, not after the bill arrives.

[Point at the five boxes]

Who I'd say it to: platform and engineering leaders at UK and EU financial firms, up to about 5,000 staff. Finance first, because they need control most: 73% of the finance job ads I read name governance as a core duty, against 41% in tech. Tech runs alongside as a side clue in the test. The trigger is a firm hiring an AI-rollout role. That's the moment someone owns the problem and has a budget, and it's visible on LinkedIn.

[Point at the positioning]

In one sentence: Kimchi is where agents work on their own inside limits set first, with budgets per team, developer and agent, the right model per task, in our cloud or yours. Unlike Claude Code or Cursor, which limit only their own tool, it covers every agent run through it, and it's switched on, not built.

[Point at the seller line]

What a seller would say: 'Your new hire gets limits, routing and cost per PR on day one, in our cloud or yours. They start on the rollout, not the plumbing.'

And to be clear: I don't position Kimchi against Claude. Claude is a model and a tool. Kimchi is where a company runs its agents, Claude included."

---

## Section 2: How I chose (about 1 minute)

[Point at the five steps]

"I worked in five steps, in this order.

**First,** I used Kimchi and read its docs, to understand what I was positioning. Its agents work on their own, and its budgets stop work at the limit, per team, developer and key.

**Second,** the market and the community: Kimchi's own Discord, Reddit and Hacker News, reviews of similar tools, and analysts. Control came up everywhere.

**Third,** competitors: who they sell to and what they say. Each tool limits only its own spend, and their ads talk about spend after it happens. None leads with team limits set first. That's the gap.

**Fourth,** job ads on LinkedIn, to see who is actually paying to solve this now, and where. That pointed to financial services in the UK and EU.

**Fifth,** I chose one main message. Kimchi could lead with several. I didn't lead with price, because only individual users cared, and they left when the free credits ended. I didn't use 'AI engineer', because Devin owns it. And I didn't lead with data sovereignty, because few buyers mention it in their own words. These stay as supporting points."

---

## Section 3: The evidence (about 2 minutes)

"The evidence follows the same order: market, competitors, then job ads.

[Point at ①]

**The market.** Across six kinds of sources, control is the only need that appears in all six. Proving the value of AI is real too, but Jellyfish and DX already sell exactly that. Model choice is Kilo's message. Price only mattered to individual users, and Kimchi's own Discord confirms it: about 1,800 members, mostly individuals who left when the credits ended. They're not the buyer. Meanwhile Gartner says AI coding bills are moving from $20–100 a seat to $2,000–5,000 per developer a month, so the pressure is growing.

[Point at ②]

**Competitors.** I asked what else this buyer could do. The most common answer is build it in-house: about half the hires are told to, often on a free gateway like LiteLLM. Claude Code, Codex and Copilot have limits, but only for their own tool. Devin sells to enterprises and banks with named customers, and it has just opened in London. Kilo is closest in features and advertises routing and cost. In their ads, DX says 'AI spend per developer went from $63 to $1,314', Jellyfish asks 'Is your AI spend paying off?', and Devin asks 'What happens to AI costs when usage doubles?' None leads with team limits set before the work runs. That's the space Kimchi can own.

[Point at ③]

**Job ads, and why they're my main reference.** Kimchi has no proven buyer yet. A job ad is the clearest public sign that a named company is paying to solve this now, in its own words. In 90 days, 998 postings came down to 73 real AI-rollout jobs: tech 40%, finance 29%. I read both groups' ads in full. Finance asks for governance far more, 73% against 41%. Tech asks more about value and speed. About half the hires are also asked to evaluate tools; Kraken's ad says 'evaluating vendors or building internally'. And 11 of the 18 finance firms hiring have up to 5,000 staff. Those 11 are cell 1 of my test.

[Point at the bottom row]

When I used Kimchi, I saw budgets per team, user and key, and cost per merged PR. I didn't see a limit stop real spend, because that needs real usage. I found no per-run cap in the docs, and running in your own cloud needs a Cast AI cluster.

So how sure am I? Medium. The need is clear, and the gap in competitors' messages is clear. What I'm least sure of is whether these firms will buy a platform or build their own, and whether control beats routing as the reason to buy. The test is designed to answer both."

---

## Section 4: What I left out (about 45 seconds)

[Point at the three columns]

"Every bet cuts something. I left out individual developers, because they left when the free credits ended. I left out finance firms over 5,000 staff, because they build their own or buy direct, like MUFG, Barclays and Citi. And the US and Middle East, because this is an EMEA role.

I won't lead with 'cheaper by X%', 'AI engineer', 'open models' or 'caps every run', because the numbers are Cast AI's own, the ground belongs to a competitor, or the product doesn't do it yet. And I skipped developer communities, X, events and cold email in Germany for this test. Each one has a condition that would bring it back.

[Point at the product box]

One thing I'd ask product for: smart budgets, limits that learn each team's normal usage and set themselves. Today every budget is set by hand. Devin shipped something similar on 19 October, so this is about keeping up, not a nice-to-have."

---

## Section 5: The test (about 2.5 minutes)

[Point at the three questions]

"The test answers two questions: which message makes finance platform leaders act, and will they buy rather than build. Plus one side clue: does tech respond better than finance?

[Point at the three ads]

Three LinkedIn ads, same image and offer, only the headline changes. A is my bet, control: 'Limits set before anyone starts.' B is pace: 'Skip the build. Start the agent rollout.' C is routing: 'Frontier models where quality matters. Lower-cost models for the rest.' C tests the reason Kimchi's only customer gives. If C wins, my bet was wrong, and I'd rather find out in four weeks than in four quarters.

[Point at the three cells]

Cell 1 is the 11 finance firms hiring now: one-to-one outreach with a one-pager built from their own job ad. Cell 2 is about 300 look-alike finance firms, where the three ads compete. Cell 3 is about 300 tech firms that see ad A only, so I can compare finance and tech on the same ad.

[Point at what they get]

When they respond, they get a one-page guide, 'Set the rules before your agents run', because a useful download converts better than a demo request. Every lead form asks one question: will you build your AI platform or buy parts of it? That measures my weakest assumption directly. The next step is a 30-minute 'compare notes' call, then a free view of their Claude Code usage, then a 30-day pilot capped by a Kimchi budget, so they feel the promise instead of reading about it. Those last two are proposals I'd confirm with product in weeks 1 and 2.

[Point at the timeline and rules]

Weeks 1 and 2 cost nothing: five to eight calls with platform leads, plus those product checks. Weeks 3 to 6 are live, for about $5,000. Week 7, I decide. The rules are set before launch. A message wins if it's about 21 clicks ahead of the next at 15,400 impressions each, which is about 90% sure for those two, and I confirm it with calls. Success means at least three technical calls from three firms, two teams connecting their usage, and one pilot scoped. If no one calls at all, I stop and recheck the pain, the buyer and the channel.

[Point at the decision table]

Every outcome has a decision, including the inconclusive one: if there aren't enough impressions to tell, I extend the top two ads for about $2,150, then decide."

---

## Close (about 30 seconds)

"If this works, I wouldn't run it by hand again. Appendix C shows how it becomes an AI process: a new job ad triggers an agent that scores the company and drafts its one-pager, with a person approving anything sent to a buyer. Appendix B shows how I used AI here, and which decisions were mine: the method, the segment, the cuts, the message and the test.

That's my bet. I'm keen to hear where you think it breaks."
