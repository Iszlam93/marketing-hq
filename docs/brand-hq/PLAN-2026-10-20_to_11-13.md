## 1 · Tue 20 Oct · Positioning is a decision, not a sentence
**Pillar:** Product marketing craft.
**Why now:** your last three posts were all about building with AI. This one shows hiring managers the PMM thinking underneath.

> Most positioning statements are written.
>
> The good ones are decided.
>
> The template is familiar: "For [who], [product] is the [category] that [value]. Unlike [alternative]…"
>
> Filling it in takes ten minutes.
>
> The hard part happens before you write a word. Three decisions:
>
> - Who it's for, and who it isn't for.
> - What the buyer compares you to. Not who you think your competitor is, but what they'd do if you didn't exist.
> - What you refuse to claim, even if it's technically true.
>
> The third one is where most teams struggle. Every feature wants a line. Every stakeholder wants their win in the message.
>
> But positioning that says everything ends up meaning nothing.
>
> The sentence is the receipt.
> The decision is the work.
>
> Which decision is hardest in your team: who it's for, or what you won't claim?

**First comment:** "The framework I lean on most for this is April Dunford's: start from competitive alternatives, not features."
**Visual (hand-drawn):** a taped card with the tab "PMM craft". Three flow chips, "Who it's for → What they compare → What you won't claim", the last one marker-orange. A Caveat note underneath: "the sentence comes last ↓".

## 3 · Tue 27 Oct · Before I publish positioning, I let AI argue against it
**Pillar:** AI for marketers.
**Why now:** a practical, save-worthy post that complements Friday's newsletter without repeating news.

> AI is a weak author but a great sparring partner.
>
> Before I publish any positioning, I run it through five prompts:
>
> 1. "Find the weakest claim and tell me why."
> 2. "Argue for our biggest competitor."
> 3. "Rewrite this for a sceptical CFO."
> 4. "Highlight every word a buyer wouldn't say."
> 5. "What would have to be true for this to be false?"
>
> Number 2 is the uncomfortable one.
>
> It's also the one that changes the message most often.
>
> I don't let AI write the positioning. I let it attack it.
>
> The thinking stays mine. The blind spots don't.
>
> Which prompt would you add to the list?

## 4 · Thu 29 Oct · A launch isn't a date. It's a readiness check.
**Pillar:** Product marketing craft.
**Why now:** launches are central to most PMM roles, and this shows real process thinking.

> A launch date isn't when the feature ships.
>
> It's when everyone who meets the customer is ready for it.
>
> I've seen good features land quietly for very ordinary reasons:
>
> - Sales heard about it the same day as customers.
> - Support had no answer for the first question.
> - The product page went live a week late.
>
> None of that is a messaging problem. It's a readiness problem.
>
> So before any launch, I check one thing per team: Product, Sales, Support, Marketing, Web, Pricing, Legal.
>
> Green, amber or red.
>
> If anything customer-facing is red, the launch isn't ready, whatever the release notes say.
>
> Ship dates are for engineering.
> Launch dates are for customers.
>
> Which team do you always wish had been ready a week earlier?

**[add: optional]** If you have a real, non-confidential example ("A feature I launched once…"), put it after line 3. It makes the post much stronger.
**First comment:** "I also tier launches (T1, T2, T3) so not every release gets the same effort. Happy to share the checklist if useful."

## 7 · Tue 10 Nov · Sales doesn't ignore battlecards. It ignores old ones.
**Pillar:** Product marketing craft.
**Why now:** a sharp, opinionated PMM take that ties to competitive intelligence and enablement.

> Sales doesn't ignore battlecards.
>
> It ignores old ones.
>
> The fastest way to lose trust is a battlecard that's wrong about pricing in a live deal.
>
> After that, reps stop opening it. And they don't tell you why.
>
> So every battlecard I build gets three things at the top:
>
> - When it was last checked.
> - Where each claim comes from.
> - What changed since the last version.
>
> And it gets checked whenever positioning changes, not once a quarter.
>
> It's less work than it sounds. Most updates are one line.
>
> Accuracy is the feature.
> Design is the wrapper.
>
> How do you keep competitive content fresh in your team?

**First comment:** "This is also why my AI marketing team flags battlecards as out of date when the foundations change. Old content is a process problem, not a writing problem."
